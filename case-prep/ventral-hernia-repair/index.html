Overview

Ventral hernia repair restores abdominal wall integrity for primary or incisional hernias. Modern coding depends on defect size, reducible versus incarcerated or strangulated status, initial versus recurrent repair, and approach. The operation is a planning case: anatomy, mesh plane, patient risk, and documentation drive outcomes.

Indications

Symptomatic ventral or incisional hernia

Enlarging defect

Incarceration or obstruction

Skin compromise or functional limitation

Selected recurrent hernia

Optimization before elective repair when possible

Contraindications

Absolute

  • Uncontrolled sepsis for elective mesh repair
  • Patient cannot tolerate anesthesia
  • Non-survivable physiology when repair is not lifesaving

Relative

  • Active smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, severe obesity, malnutrition
  • Contaminated field requiring altered mesh strategy
  • Loss of domain needing preoperative planning
  • Poorly optimized ascites or immunosuppression

Anatomy Review

Linea alba and anterior/posterior rectus sheath

Rectus muscle and neurovascular bundles

Preperitoneal, retrorectus, intraperitoneal, and onlay planes

Hernia sac, fascial edges, and multiple swiss-cheese defects

Semilunar line and lateral abdominal wall

Operative Steps

Preop planning

What: Assess defect size, location, recurrence, contamination, and patient optimization.

Why: Plan approach and mesh plane.

Pitfalls: Ignoring optimization increases recurrence/infection.

Exposure/access

What: Open, laparoscopic, or robotic access based on case.

Why: Safe entry avoids bowel injury.

Pitfalls: Adhesions under prior incision are common.

Adhesiolysis/reduction

What: Free contents and reduce hernia sac.

Why: Defines defect and protects bowel.

Pitfalls: Thermal or traction injury to bowel is a major risk.

Defect measurement

What: Measure total defect width/length after reduction.

Why: CPT selection depends on documented size.

Pitfalls: Using skin bulge instead of fascial defect is wrong.

Plane creation

What: Create selected mesh plane.

Why: Mesh position affects recurrence and complications.

Pitfalls: Lateral dissection can injure neurovascular supply.

Fascial closure/mesh

What: Close fascia when appropriate and place mesh with adequate overlap/fixation.

Why: Restores abdominal wall and lowers recurrence.

Pitfalls: Bridging without reason increases recurrence.

Hemostasis/closure

What: Manage dead space, drains if used, close layers.

Why: Prevents hematoma/seroma and wound problems.

Pitfalls: Tension and devascularized flaps cause complications.

Decision Pearls

Scenario: multiple small midline defects are found instead of one defect. Reduce contents, define all fascial defects, measure the total repaired defect area according to current coding rules, choose a mesh strategy with adequate overlap, and document the measurement clearly.

Common Complications

Seroma: recognize fluid collection; prevent by dead-space management; observe or drain if symptomatic/infected.

SSI/mesh infection: recognize erythema, drainage, systemic signs; prevent by optimization and sterile technique; manage with antibiotics, drainage, or mesh removal when necessary.

Bowel injury: recognize enterotomy or postop sepsis; prevent with careful adhesiolysis; repair immediately when identified.

Recurrence: prevent with optimization, adequate overlap, closure, and plane selection; manage based on symptoms and risk.

Chronic pain: prevent nerve-aware fixation; manage with multimodal therapy and evaluation.

Documentation Pearls

Educational guidance only. This section is not a complete dictated operative note and should not be copied into the chart.

  • Document initial versus recurrent hernia.
  • Document reducible, incarcerated, or strangulated status.
  • Document total fascial defect size after reduction.
  • Document approach and mesh plane/type when used.
  • Document adhesions, contamination, bowel injury/repair, component separation, or TAR if performed.

What Gets Residents In Trouble

  • Confusing tissue planes.
  • Inadequate mesh overlap.
  • Failure to appreciate fascial tension.
  • Poor defect measurement.
  • Ignoring contamination after enterotomy.

When Things Are Not Going According To Plan

  • Posterior sheath destroyed: repair layer, convert to TAR, choose alternate plane, stage, or abort reconstruction.
  • Bowel injury: repair/source control and reconsider mesh strategy.
  • Tension too high: release, stage, or change plan.
  • Loss of domain physiology: stop closure and protect the patient.

Surgeon's Pearl

Most residents focus on the hernia. Experienced surgeons focus on the abdominal wall.

Coding Pearls

Common Documentation and Coding Mistakes

  • Failure to document defect size.
  • Failure to document recurrent status.
  • Failure to document incarceration or strangulation.
  • Unsupported component separation documentation.
  • Failure to document mesh plane and contamination.

The Five Things To Know Before Scrubbing In

  • Measure the fascial defect after reduction.
  • Know initial versus recurrent and reducible versus incarcerated.
  • Mesh plane determines risk.
  • Posterior sheath integrity matters.
  • A forced closure is not a durable repair.
