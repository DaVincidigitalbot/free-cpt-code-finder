How One Flap Can More Than Double the wRVUs of a Below-Knee Amputation

Last reviewed: May 2026

Graydon Stallard, DO, FACOS, FACS • Updated May 2026 • 6 min read

Most surgeons think of a below-knee amputation as a below-knee amputation. The payer does not. And frankly, neither should you.

One of the biggest mistakes surgeons make is assuming that every amputation is valued the same way. In reality, the work involved in creating a durable, functional, prosthetic-ready residual limb can be dramatically different from simply removing the leg and closing the skin.

The difference is not a coding loophole.

The difference is surgery.

The numbers

Procedure CPT code Physician wRVUs
Below-knee amputation 27880 14.99
Lower extremity myocutaneous/fasciocutaneous flap 15738 18.56
Combined total 27880 + 15738 33.55

Read that again.

The flap procedure alone carries 18.56 wRVUs, which is actually more physician work than the amputation itself.

When combined, the total physician work increases from 14.99 wRVUs to 33.55 wRVUs. That is a 123.8% increase in physician work RVUs.

Not because somebody found a trick.

Because the surgeon performed significantly more surgery.

Important: separate reporting depends on the actual operative work performed, payer rules, bundling edits, documentation, and local coding/compliance review. The point is not to force a code onto every BKA. The point is to document real reconstruction when real reconstruction was performed.

A BKA is not always just a BKA

Anyone can remove a leg.

Creating a residual limb that will tolerate years of prosthetic use is an entirely different challenge.

Many patients requiring lower extremity amputation present with:

  • Severe infection
  • Osteomyelitis
  • Trauma
  • Prior failed operations
  • Peripheral vascular disease
  • Charcot deformity
  • Significant soft tissue loss

These patients often require far more than a standard closure.

They require reconstruction.

That may involve:

  • Muscle mobilization
  • Flap creation
  • Soft tissue advancement
  • Coverage of exposed bone
  • Formal myodesis techniques
  • Reconstruction of a durable weight-bearing stump

That is real work. And it matters.

The hardest part is not taking the leg off

The hardest part of many amputations is creating a functional limb afterward.

The goal is not simply to get the patient through the operation.

The goal is to help them walk.

A poorly constructed residual limb can result in:

  • Prosthetic intolerance
  • Skin breakdown
  • Chronic pain
  • Recurrent ulceration
  • Revision surgery

A durable residual limb can dramatically improve long-term function and quality of life.

That reconstruction takes time. It takes planning. And it takes technical skill.

The documentation mistake surgeons make

Here is where many surgeons accidentally sabotage themselves.

They spend an additional hour performing complex reconstruction and then dictate:

Flaps were fashioned and secured.

That is it. One sentence.

The operative note equivalent of:

Some surgery happened.

Imagine documenting a Whipple procedure with:

Abdominal contents rearranged.

Or a CABG with:

Heart plumbing improved.

The coder reviewing your note has no way to appreciate the complexity of what was actually performed.

If you performed it, document it

When performing flap reconstruction or formal myodesis during an amputation, the operative note should clearly describe:

  • Why the reconstruction was necessary
  • The tissues mobilized
  • The flap design
  • Coverage of exposed bone
  • Muscle advancement techniques
  • How the reconstruction improved durability and function
  • The final residual limb construct

The documentation should allow another surgeon to understand exactly what happened in the operating room.

Because if the work is not documented, the work effectively does not exist.

The bottom line

A below-knee amputation requiring flap reconstruction is not the same operation as a straightforward below-knee amputation.

It requires more planning. More technical skill. More operative time. And substantially more physician work.

In this example, when CPT 15738 is separately supported, the physician work value increases from 14.99 wRVUs to 33.55 wRVUs, more than doubling the total value of the case.

That is not upcoding.

That is accurately reflecting the complexity of the operation actually performed.

Because if you are spending an extra hour mobilizing tissue, performing a formal myodesis, creating flap coverage, and building a prosthetic-ready limb, your operative note should not read like you clipped a toenail.
